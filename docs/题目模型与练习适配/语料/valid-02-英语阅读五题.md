# Reading Comprehension — Passage 3

::::material{id=mat-1 title="The Quiet Revolution in Urban Farming"}
For most of the twentieth century, food traveled one way: from rural fields to city tables. That assumption is now being quietly reversed. In warehouses on the outskirts of Singapore, lettuce grows under LED lights stacked twelve metres high; on rooftops in Brooklyn, beekeepers harvest honey metres above rush-hour traffic. Urban farming, long dismissed as a hobby for enthusiasts, has become a serious industry.

The economics are more subtle than headlines suggest. Vertical farms consume electricity at rates that would have horrified farmers a generation ago, yet the price of LED lighting has fallen by roughly ninety percent since 2010, and renewable contracts increasingly cover the difference. Water is the sharper advantage: closed-loop hydroponic systems recycle up to ninety-five percent of their water, a figure traditional irrigation cannot approach. In arid, import-dependent cities such as Dubai, that arithmetic matters more than any ideology.

Critics raise a fair objection — calories. Leafy greens and herbs, the crops indoor farms favour, are profitable but nutritionally light; no vertical farm feeds a city on spinach. Researchers respond that the industry should be judged as a complement rather than a substitute: fresh produce near consumers cuts transport losses, which currently claim a third of tropical fruits shipped long distances.

The social effects may outlast the technological ones. Studies of community gardens in Detroit and Havana found that neighbourhoods with shared growing plots reported stronger informal networks — people lent tools, watched each other's children and voted more often. Whether warehouse farms, with their investor pitch decks, can reproduce that civic glue is an open question. A farm that employs six technicians may feed a hundred thousand people, but it does not teach a hundred thousand people to know their neighbours.

::::question{id=rc-1 type=choice difficulty=2 knowledge="main idea"}
The primary purpose of the passage is to

- [ ] celebrate the triumph of vertical farming over traditional agriculture
- [x] assess urban farming's economics, limits and social effects in a balanced way
- [ ] argue that cities should grow all of their own food
- [ ] explain why LED lighting prices collapsed after 2010

:::solution
The passage weighs advantages (water, proximity) against limits (calories, civic value) — a balanced assessment, not advocacy.
:::

::::question{id=rc-2 type=choice difficulty=3 knowledge="detail comprehension"}
According to Paragraph 2, the sharpest economic advantage of hydroponic urban farms is

- [ ] cheap LED lighting
- [ ] lower labour costs
- [x] water recycling of up to 95%
- [ ] free renewable electricity

:::solution
"Water is the sharper advantage" — lighting is described as improved but secondary.
:::

::::question{id=rc-3 type=choice difficulty=3 knowledge="inference"}
The phrase "that arithmetic" (Paragraph 2) refers to

- [ ] the cost of shipping lettuce to Singapore
- [ ] the balance between water savings and import dependence
- [x] the calculation that makes water efficiency decisive for arid cities
- [ ] the profits of rooftop beekeeping

:::solution
In Dubai's context, water arithmetic — not ideology — is what makes urban farming rational.

::::question{id=rc-4 type=multi difficulty=4 knowledge="evaluating claims"}
Which of the following statements are supported by the passage?

- [x] Transport losses affect a large share of tropical fruits moved over long distances
- [ ] Urban farms have already replaced rural suppliers in Singapore
- [x] Researchers see urban farms as complements to, not replacements for, rural agriculture
- [ ] Community gardens increase voter turnout in all cities studied

:::solution
A third of tropical fruits are lost (supported); "complement rather than substitute" is explicit. Replacement claims are contradicted or overstated ("all cities" overreaches).
:::
